FitTrack AI is a food, training and body-weight diary where you chat in Portuguese — “I ate 150 g of rice, 100 g of beans and 180 g of chicken breast”, “I ran 5 km in 30 minutes”, “am I in a deficit today?” — and get back numbers computed from an official food-composition table. Everything runs on your own computer: Python, Streamlit and a local SQLite database, with optional local AI through Ollama. The code is on GitHub.

This is not an installation guide (the README covers that). It is about the decisions that make an assistant like this trustworthy: where the AI comes in, where it must not, and the calculation details that usually go unnoticed.

Disclaimer. Table calories and equation-based energy expenditure are population estimates. The app does not diagnose anything and does not replace a dietitian or physician.

§1The problem: LLMs are great at understanding text and terrible at inventing numbers

Asking a chatbot “how many calories are in my lunch?” works surprisingly well — until it doesn't. The model may put 180 g of chicken at 250 kcal one day and 330 the next, “remember” a value that is in no table, or say it logged something it did not. For a diary that will add up months of data, that is unacceptable.

The project's rule: the AI only turns text into structure; every number comes from deterministic code and sourced data.

message ─► parsing (PT-BR rules  or  optional Ollama)
        ─► validated JSON (Pydantic)  ─► "grounding" in the original text
        ─► TACO lookup + unit conversion + METs  (Python)
        ─► editable DRAFT  ─► explicit confirmation  ─► SQLite
question ─► analytics engine (pandas over the database) ─► templated answer

§2Two parsers, one contract

There is a rule-based parser for Portuguese (regular expressions, spelled-out numbers, “half a bread roll”, “1h20”, “pace 5:30/km”, dates such as “yesterday” and “12/11”) that works offline with no model at all. When Ollama is on, it gets the same message and must return JSON in the same schema — using structured output at temperature 0.

Then comes the step that matters most, grounding: every quantity, duration or weight the LLM returned must appear in the user's message (directly or through an obvious conversion such as 1 h → 60 min). If it does not, the value is dropped. Foods that are not in the text are removed. Whatever is missing is filled in by the rules or asked.

MessageLLM returnedAfter grounding
“comi arroz e 2 ovos e corri 5 km” (rice, 2 eggs, ran 5 km)rice 150 g · eggs 2 · banana 1 · run 30 min, 5 kmrice (how much?) · eggs 2 · run (how long?), 5 km

Nothing is saved without going through a draft that shows each chosen food, the grams, the conversion rule used and the nutrients — and it only reaches the database after “yes” or the confirm button.

§3The food database: TACO extracted from the official PDF

The source is TACO, 4th edition (the Brazilian food-composition table, NEPA/UNICAMP, 2011), whose publication allows reproduction with attribution. Because it ships as a PDF, I wrote an extractor that reads words with their positions and assigns each number to the nearest column — necessary because empty cells simply do not appear in the text, and a line-by-line read misaligns everything. Result: 597 foods with IDs 1–597 in sequence, checked for Atwater consistency (4/4/9 kcal per gram). Six items that TACO itself marks as not analysed — including, curiously, whole cow's milk — were not imported: the app asks you to enter them from the label instead of making them up.

Turning “2 bread rolls” or “200 ml of milk” into grams is where many apps guess. Here:

UnitRule
g, kg, mgdirect
ml, lonly with a known density for that food
unit, slice, ladle, pot…only with a household measure registered for that food (shown in the draft)
anything elsethe assistant asks for grams

When a name is ambiguous (“leite” matches coconut milk, goat's milk, condensed milk…), the app does not pick for you: it shows the options.

§4Energy expenditure without counting the workout twice

Basal metabolic rate comes from Mifflin-St Jeor. The classic mistake in diet apps is adding a workout's gross energy to a daily expenditure that already includes rest. One hour of weight training at 5 METs for 80 kg “burns” 400 gross kcal — but 80 of them you would have burned anyway, sitting still.

daily expenditure = BMR × 1.2  +  Σ (MET − 1) × weight × hours
                      baseline       only the workout's NET energy

There is an alternative method (BMR × activity-level factor, without adding workouts) for people who do not log everything. METs come from the Compendium of Physical Activities; for running, walking and cycling with distance and time, the MET is interpolated by speed.

§5A day without logs is not a deficit day

If you forgot to log Tuesday's food, a naive calculation sees 0 intake and a 2,000 kcal “deficit” — which contaminates the weekly average and the running balance. In FitTrack, days without food logs simply have no balance: they show as “not logged” and stay out of the averages.

The same care applies to weight. Daily swings are water, salt and glycogen; the app shows a 7-day moving average and a regression trend, and compares the observed change with the one expected from the computed balance. With enough data (≥ 8 weigh-ins over ≥ 14 days and ≥ 10 logged days), it estimates the expenditure implied by the real trend and warns when the app's estimate looks too high or too low — without turning an accumulated deficit into a promise of fat loss.

§6Questions answered from the database

“How much can I still eat today?”, “compare this week with last week”, “which days was I over my target?”, “am I eating enough protein?”. Each question maps to a query in the analytics engine (pandas over SQLite) and the answer is built from a template. The LLM never writes numbers — so the number in the sentence is, by construction, the number in the database.

§7Your data stays yours

  • Local SQLite with versioned migrations, foreign keys and range constraints (a 5 kg body weight is rejected by the database itself).
  • Every meal item stores a snapshot of its nutrients at logging time.
  • A per-day fingerprint against accidental duplicates (confirming twice does not log twice).
  • Change history, automatic backup, restore with a safety copy, and CSV export.
  • Technical logs without message contents.

§8Tests

There are 137 tests: units, calculations, no double counting, moving averages, persistence after closing and reopening the database, duplicates, ambiguity, history queries checked against known values, grounding of LLM output, rendering of every page with Streamlit's AppTest, and two tests against a real Ollama (skipped when it is not running). They found real bugs — “almocei arroz” (I had rice for lunch) turning into “rroz”, “bike ergométrica” classified as outdoor cycling, a future date silently moved to the previous year — all documented in the repository.
